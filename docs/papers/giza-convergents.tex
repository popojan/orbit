\begin{abstract}
The three pyramids at Giza exhibit remarkable number-theoretic patterns in their dimensional ratios. We demonstrate that their height-to-base ratios correspond to three consecutive convergents of $\sqrt{\varphi}/2$, where $\varphi = (1+\sqrt{5})/2$ is the golden ratio. Furthermore, we show that the dimensions of Khufu's pyramid encode the fundamental solution to a Pell equation and form a Dedekind cut approximating $\sqrt{7/11}$.
\end{abstract}

\section{The Golden Pyramid}

Consider a pyramid with a square base of side length $2$ and slant height equal to the golden ratio $\varphi$. We call this the \emph{golden pyramid} or \emph{Kepler pyramid}.

\begin{proposition}[Height-to-base ratio of golden pyramid]
If a pyramid has unit half-base and slant height $\varphi$, then its height-to-base ratio is $\sqrt{\varphi}/2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $h$ denote the height and consider the right triangle formed by the half-base (length 1), height $h$, and slant height $\varphi$.

By the Pythagorean theorem:
\begin{align*}
1^2 + h^2 &= \varphi^2 \\
h^2 &= \varphi^2 - 1 \\
h^2 &= \varphi \quad \text{(since $\varphi^2 = \varphi + 1$)} \\
h &= \sqrt{\varphi}
\end{align*}

Therefore, the height-to-base ratio is:
\[
\frac{h}{b} = \frac{h}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{\varphi}}{2} \approx 0.6360098\ldots \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

This ratio defines the geometric ideal for a pyramid whose slant edges follow the golden proportion. The question of whether the golden ratio was deliberately incorporated into ancient pyramids has been extensively debated \cite{livio2002}.

\section{The Convergent Structure}

\begin{theorem}[Giza pyramids as convergents]
The ratios 2/3, 5/8, and 7/11 are three consecutive convergents of the continued fraction expansion of $\sqrt{\varphi}/2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The continued fraction of $\sqrt{\varphi}/2$ is:
\[
\sqrt{\varphi}/2 = [0; 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 1, 1, 4, \ldots]
\]

Computing successive convergents:
\begin{align*}
p_0/q_0 &= 0/1 \\
p_1/q_1 &= 1/1 \\
p_2/q_2 &= 1/2 \\
p_3/q_3 &= 2/3 \quad \text{\textbf{(Khafre)}} \\
p_4/q_4 &= 5/8 \quad \text{\textbf{(Menkaure)}} \\
p_5/q_5 &= 7/11 \quad \text{\textbf{(Khufu)}} \\
p_6/q_6 &= 159/250
\end{align*}

These convergents provide the best rational approximations to $\sqrt{\varphi}/2$ at each level of denominator complexity.
\end{proof}

\centering

\midrule

5/8 & 0.625 & 1.1\% & Menkaure \\

\begin{remark}
Khufu's pyramid was constructed \emph{first} (c. 2580--2560 BCE) and uses the \emph{best} approximation (7/11, error 0.056\%). The later pyramids—Khafre (c. 2520--2494 BCE) and Menkaure (c. 2490--2472 BCE)—use cruder convergents (2/3 and 5/8, errors 4.8\% and 1.7\% respectively). This chronological pattern suggests the first pyramid achieved optimal precision, and later constructions represent simplified or scaled-down implementations rather than progressive refinement.
\end{remark}

\section{The Brahmagupta--Bhaskara Equation Connection}

The dimensions of Khufu's pyramid reveal an even deeper number-theoretic structure.

\subsection{Fundamental Solution to the Varga Prakriti}

Consider the scaling of Khufu's dimensions from the reduced ratio 7/11:
\begin{align*}
\text{Height:} \quad 280 &= 7 \times 40 \\
\text{Base:} \quad 440 &= 11 \times 40
\end{align*}

The modulus is 40 cubits $\approx$ 21 meters. Now scale the ratio $7/11$ to have denominator 440:
\[
\frac{7}{11} = \frac{280}{440} = \frac{351 \cdot 280/351}{440}
\]

This gives the pair $(x, y) = (351, 440)$ where $y = 11 \times 40$.

\begin{theorem}[Khufu dimensions solve the Brahmagupta--Bhaskara equation]
The pair $(x, y) = (351, 11 \times 40) = (351, 440)$ is the fundamental integer solution to the Diophantine equation of type studied by Brahmagupta (628 CE) and Bhaskara II (1150 CE), called \emph{varga prakriti} (square-nature) in Sanskrit:
\[
x^2 - \frac{7}{11} y^2 = 1
\]
This equation type is sometimes called ``Pell's equation'' due to Euler's misattribution to John Pell (17th century), though Pell made no significant contributions to its solution.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Direct computation:
\begin{align*}
351^2 - \frac{7}{11} \cdot 440^2 &= 123201 - \frac{7}{11} \cdot 193600 \\
&= 123201 - 7 \cdot 17600 \\
&= 123201 - 123200 \\
&= 1
\end{align*}

To verify this is fundamental, note that $\gcd(351, 440) = 1$ and any smaller solution would have $x < 351$. The continued fraction algorithm for $\sqrt{7/11}$ produces this as the first non-trivial solution. Bhaskara's \emph{chakravala} (cyclic) method, developed in the 12th century, provides an efficient algorithm for finding such solutions.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Dedekind Cut Property}

The pyramid dimensions themselves form bounds that approximate $\sqrt{7/11}$.

\begin{proposition}[Pyramid dimensions as Dedekind cut]
The ratios formed by Khufu's height and base bracket $\sqrt{7/11}$:
\[
\frac{280}{351} < \sqrt{\frac{7}{11}} < \frac{351}{440}
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Computing the three values:
\begin{align*}
\frac{280}{351} &= 0.7977207977\ldots \\
\sqrt{\frac{7}{11}} &= 0.7977240352\ldots \\
\frac{351}{440} &= 0.7977272727\ldots
\end{align*}

Both inequalities hold:
\begin{itemize}
\item $280/351 = 0.79772080\ldots < 0.79772404\ldots = \sqrt{7/11}$ (lower bound)
\item $\sqrt{7/11} = 0.79772404\ldots < 0.79772727\ldots = 351/440$ (upper bound)
\end{itemize}

The pyramid dimensions provide tight bounds for $\sqrt{7/11}$, with errors of $0.0004\%$ (lower) and $0.00004\%$ (upper).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This bracketing property shows that the pyramid dimensions $(280, 440)$ encode not only the rational approximation $7/11$ to $\sqrt{\varphi}/2$, but also provide a Dedekind cut for $\sqrt{7/11}$ when normalized by the Pell solution component $351$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Summary of Khufu's Number-Theoretic Properties}

The dimensions of the Great Pyramid simultaneously encode:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Convergent approximation:} $7/11$ is the 6th convergent of $\sqrt{\varphi}/2$, giving 0.056\% error
\item \textbf{Diophantine solution:} $(351, 440)$ is the fundamental integer solution to $x^2 - \frac{7}{11}y^2 = 1$
\item \textbf{Dedekind cut:} $\frac{280}{351} < \sqrt{\frac{7}{11}} < \frac{351}{440}$ brackets the algebraic number with $0.0004\%$ precision
\item \textbf{Simple modulus:} Both dimensions are multiples of 40 cubits ($\approx$21 m)
\end{enumerate}

\section{Measurement Methodology and Uncertainty}

\subsection{Historical Context: Petrie's Survey (1880--1883)}

Sir William Matthew Flinders Petrie's survey of the Giza plateau, published in \emph{The Pyramids and Temples of Gizeh} (1883), remains the foundation for pyramid metrology. Petrie traveled to Egypt intending to verify Charles Piazzi Smyth's theory that biblical prophecy was encoded in pyramid measurements. His meticulous work instead \emph{disproved} Smyth's "pyramid inch" hypothesis, establishing modern archaeological standards.

\subsection{Height Determination}

The original pyramid heights cannot be measured directly—the capstones have been missing since antiquity. Heights are calculated from:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Remaining casing stone fragments (determining slope)
\item Base measurements (directly measured)
\item Pythagorean extrapolation: $h = (b/2) \tan\theta$
\end{enumerate}

For Khufu, the estimated height is approximately 5776 inches (280 cubits), yielding a slope angle of $51.867^\circ \pm 0.036^\circ$ (1$\sigma$).

\subsection{Independent Verification: King's Chamber}

The King's Chamber provides crucial independent evidence. Located deep within the pyramid, its polished granite walls are not subject to weathering:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Length:} 412.24" = 20 cubits (exactly)
\item \textbf{Width:} 206.12" = 10 cubits (exactly)
\item \textbf{Height:} 230.09" = $\sqrt{125}$ = $5\sqrt{5}$ cubits (11.18 cubits)
\end{itemize}

The body diagonal equals exactly 25 cubits, forming a 3:4:5 Pythagorean triple scaled by 5. This provides direct evidence that the Egyptians worked with $\sqrt{5}$, independent of exterior slope measurements.

\subsection{Methodological Circularity}

Petrie's methodology contains potential circularity:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Assume Egyptians designed in whole cubits
\item Measure structures in inches
\item Calculate cubit length to yield round numbers
\item Apply to other structures
\end{enumerate}

However, this circularity is broken by agreement with surviving cubit sticks ($\pm$1\% variation around 524 mm), internal chamber dimensions (King's Chamber: 10$\times$20$\times$$5\sqrt{5}$), consistency across dozens of independent structures, and modern GPS verification \cite{dash2015} showing agreement within 0.05\%.

\subsection{Chronological Gap}

The surviving cubit rods date from the 18th--22nd Dynasties (circa 1350--850 BCE), approximately 1000 years \emph{after} the pyramids. However, these later rods show "mistakes" and "clumsily drawn graduations" \cite{monnier2016}, suggesting they are ceremonial copies of an earlier, more precise standard. The pyramid's construction precision (0.1\%) exceeds rod variation (1\%), consistent with a lost master standard.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation}

The convergent structure presents a tension between two perspectives:

\textbf{If the ratios arose independently,} the pattern is extraordinary. The probability that three ratios chosen without reference to each other would all be consecutive convergents of $\sqrt{\varphi}/2$ is vanishingly small. The additional property that Khufu's dimensions encode the fundamental solution to a Brahmagupta--Bhaskara equation compounds the improbability. Under this interpretation, the pattern suggests either remarkable coincidence or sophisticated mathematical knowledge.

\textbf{If builders sought simple fractions near a target slope,} the convergent structure is expected rather than surprising. The seked system naturally produces simple fractions, and structurally optimal slopes fall in the range 5--5.6 palms. In this range, the simplest rational approximations with small denominators are precisely 2/3, 5/8, and 7/11—the convergents emerge inevitably as the best approximations. Under this interpretation, the pattern reflects practical construction constraints rather than number-theoretic sophistication.

The historical context complicates both interpretations. The pyramids were constructed circa 2580--2510 BCE, predating documented knowledge of the golden ratio $\varphi$ (first rigorously studied by Euclid c. 300 BCE), continued fractions (developed by Bombelli, 1572), and Diophantine equations of type $x^2 - Dy^2 = 1$ seeking integer solutions (Diophantus c. 250 CE; systematically solved by Brahmagupta, 628 CE; Bhaskara II's \emph{chakravala} method, 1150 CE; Fermat, 1657; Euler, 1732) \cite{mactutor-pell}. These massive construction projects—each requiring decades and involving thousands of workers—represent deliberate architectural design rather than experimentation.

Surviving Egyptian mathematical texts on papyrus \cite{rhind,vymazalova2006} served primarily administrative and pedagogical purposes—teaching scribes calculations for taxation, grain storage, and construction logistics. If higher mathematical knowledge existed, it may have been encoded directly in monumental architecture rather than in perishable documents. The sophistication of the King's Chamber dimensions ($5\sqrt{5}$ cubits height) suggests geometric knowledge beyond what appears in hieratic mathematical texts, though this does not definitively establish whether builders understood the convergent structure as such or arrived at it through other means.

\subsection{The Seked System}

The Egyptians measured pyramid slopes using \emph{seked}: the horizontal displacement (in palms) per cubit of vertical rise \cite{rossi2004}. One royal cubit equals 7 palms.

For Khufu's pyramid:
\[
\text{seked} = \frac{220 \text{ cubits (half-base)}}{280 \text{ cubits (height)}} \times 7 \text{ palms/cubit} = 5.5 \text{ palms}
\]

This equals exactly 5 palms + 2 digits (where 1 palm = 4 digits), a simple measurement achievable with rope and surveying tools.

\section{Conclusion}

The three pyramids at Giza exhibit a convergent structure, with height-to-base ratios corresponding to successive best rational approximations to the golden pyramid ratio $\sqrt{\varphi}/2$. The dimensions of Khufu's pyramid additionally encode the fundamental solution to a Brahmagupta--Bhaskara equation and form a Dedekind cut approximating $\sqrt{7/11}$.

Whether this structure reflects sophisticated number-theoretic knowledge, practical seked constraints naturally producing simple fractions, or some combination of both, cannot be definitively determined from the archaeological record. What remains observable is the mathematical coherence of the dimensional relationships and the precision with which ancient Egyptian architects executed their designs.
