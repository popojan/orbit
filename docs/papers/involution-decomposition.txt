Atomic Involution Decomposition of Calkin–Wilf Generators
Jan Popelka
December 2025
Draft v0.1

Abstract
We show that the Calkin–Wilf tree generators L(x) = x/(1 + x) and R(x) = x + 1 can be
decomposed into compositions of three elementary Möbius involutions: σ(x) = (1 − x)/(1 + x),
κ(x) = 1 − x, and ι(x) = 1/x. Specifically, L = κικσισ and R = κσισ. This decomposition
reveals that the transitive action of ⟨L, R⟩ on Q+ can be generated by involutions with coefficients
in {−1, 0, 1} only. We also identify the orbit structure of the subgroup ⟨σ, κ⟩ restricted to
(0, 1) ∩ Q, which is governed by the invariant I(p/q) = odd(p(q − p)), where odd(n) = n/2v2 (n)
denotes the odd part of n.

Orbit Structure of ⟨σ, κ⟩

While the full group ⟨σ, κ, ι⟩ acts transitively on Q+ , the subgroup ⟨σ, κ⟩ has a richer orbit structure
when restricted to (0, 1) ∩ Q.
Proposition 5 (Orbit Invariant). For p/q ∈ (0, 1) ∩ Q in lowest terms, define
I(p/q) = odd(p(q − p)),
where odd(n) = n/2ν2 (n) is the odd part of n. Then I is invariant under σ and κ.
Proof. For κ: κ(p/q) = (q − p)/q, so I(κ(p/q)) = odd((q − p) · p) = I(p/q).
For σ: σ(p/q) = (q−p)/(q+p), so I(σ(p/q)) = odd((q−p)·2p) = odd(2p(q−p)) = odd(p(q−p)) =
I(p/q).
Theorem 6 (Orbit Structure). Two fractions in (0, 1) are in the same ⟨σ, κ⟩-orbit if and only if
they have the same orbit signature {A, B}, where A and B are defined below.
Proof. Consider the logit coordinate y = x/(1 − x) for x ∈ (0, 1). For p/q in lowest terms, write
y = p/(q − p) and decompose:

Key observation: The operations σκ and κσ only change (α, β) by scaling, while κ swaps
(A, B) ↔ (B, A). No operation changes the unordered pair {A, B}.
Therefore, the orbit signature {A, B} is a complete invariant: two fractions are in the same orbit
iff they have the same {A, B}.
Canonical form: Each orbit has a unique representative with α = β = 0, i.e., y = A/B,
corresponding to fraction A/(A + B).
Corollary 7 (Number of Orbits). For invariant I = pa11 · · · pakk , the number of distinct ⟨σ, κ⟩-orbits
with this invariant equals 2k−1 , corresponding to unordered coprime factorizations I = A · B with
gcd(A, B) = 1. (Each prime power pai i goes entirely to A or B, giving 2k choices modulo the swap
(A, B) ∼ (B, A).)
Example 8. For prime I = 3: only one factorization {1, 3}, hence one orbit. Canonical: 1/4 (or
κ-related 3/4).
For composite I = 15 = 3 × 5: two factorizations {1, 15} and {3, 5}, hence two distinct orbits:
• Orbit {1, 15}: contains 1/16, 15/16, 2/17, . . . (canonical: 1/16)
• Orbit {3, 5}: contains 3/8, 5/8, 3/13, 5/11, . . . (canonical: 3/8)
These orbits have the same I = 15 but are not connected by σ, κ.

Remark 10 (Generic Path Length). For typical rationals, no systematic speedup is guaranteed. By
Khinchin’s theorem [3], the geometric mean of CF coefficients converges to K0 ≈ 2.685 for almost
all reals, so a random p/q has CF sum ≈ c log q (with c depending on the distribution). In this
generic regime, the involution path length is also O(log q)—neither representation is asymptotically
superior.
The 1/(2k + 1) family achieves exponential speedup precisely because its CF representation
[0; 1, 2k ] has a single large coefficient. Similarly, fractions near quadratic surds (with eventually
periodic CFs) may benefit from involution shortcuts. The involution approach thus offers advantages
for structured fractions, not random ones.

Connection to Stern–Brocot Tree

The Calkin–Wilf tree is closely related to the Stern–Brocot tree [2]. Both enumerate Q+ using
binary tree structures. Wildberger [4] studied L-R factorizations in the context of Pell equations
and the Stern–Brocot tree.
Our contribution is the observation that L and R themselves factor into elementary involutions,
providing a finer decomposition than the L-R level.

Corollary 19 (Word Problem). The word problem for ⟨σ, κ, ι⟩ is solvable in linear time O(n),
where n is the word length.
Proof. Since the only relations are σ 2 = κ2 = ι2 = 1, the normal form of any word is obtained
by removing adjacent identical letters. This can be done in a single left-to-right pass: maintain a
stack, and for each new letter, either push it (if different from top) or pop (if same as top). The
resulting word has no adjacent identical letters.
Remark 20 (Chomsky Classification). The reduction rules σσ → ε, κκ → ε, ιι → ε form a contextfree grammar. Unlike typical CFGs (which require O(n3 ) parsing via CYK), our specific grammar
admits linear-time parsing because the rules are purely local (adjacent pairs only).
The Calkin–Wilf generators have fixed-length representations:
• R = κσισ (4 symbols)
• L = κικσισ (6 symbols)
Both are already in normal form (alternating letters). Hence the atomic representation of any CW
path has length 4nR + 6nL , where nR and nL are the numbers of R and L steps respectively.

Discussion

The decomposition of Calkin–Wilf generators into elementary involutions provides a finer-grained
view of rational number arithmetic, with {σ, κ, ι} serving as atomic building blocks.

Significance of the Decomposition
Why decompose L and R into involutions? Several perspectives illuminate the value of this viewpoint:
1. Geometric interpretation. The three involutions have natural geometric meanings: ι is
inversion about 1 (the point swapping (0, ∞)), κ is reflection about 1/2 (the midpoint of
(0, 1)), and σ is the real Cayley transform, conjugating hyperbolic/trigonometric functions
to exponentials (cf. Open Question 6). The Calkin–Wilf generators inherit these geometric
properties as compositions.

7

2. Coxeter structure. The group ⟨σ, κ, ι⟩ is an infinite Coxeter group (Proposition 17), generated by reflections with all products of distinct generators having infinite order. This places
the rational enumeration problem in the broader context of Coxeter-theoretic combinatorics,
where word-length and normal forms have rich theory.
3. Minimal coefficients. The involutions σ, κ, ι use only coefficients from {−1, 0, 1}. This is
computationally significant: multiplication and division in the group action reduce to additions
and sign changes, enabling efficient symbolic and numerical implementation.
4. Orbit invariants. The subgroup ⟨σ, κ⟩ admits a non-trivial orbit invariant I(p/q) = odd(p(q−
p)), partitioning (0, 1) ∩ Q into infinitely many orbits. The full group’s transitivity arises precisely because ι connects these orbits.
Open questions:
(1) The group ⟨σ, κ, ι⟩ is an infinite Coxeter group. What is its relationship to other well-known
Coxeter groups?
(2) Can the invariant I be generalized to characterize orbits of other involution subgroups?
(3) For fractions within the same I-orbit, can the ⟨σ, κ⟩ path be computed directly, without
enumerating the orbit?
(4) The involution σ appears to simplify continued fraction representations. In a companion paper [5], we establish a bijection between CF coefficients and Egyptian fraction decompositions.
Does the action of ⟨σ, κ⟩ on rationals have a natural interpretation in terms of this CF–Egypt
correspondence?
(5) The involution σ extends naturally to irrational numbers. Preliminary computations suggest
remarkable behavior on transcendental constants:
√
• σ fixes 2 − 1 (whose CF is [0; 2]).
√
• σ maps 1/φ = ( 5 − 1)/2 (CF [0; 1]) to a number with CF [0; 4].
• σ(e − 2) = (3 − e)/(e − 1) = coth(1/2) − 2 has CF [0; 6, 10, 14, . . .].
The last identity follows from coth(1/2) = (e + 1)/(e − 1) = [2; 6, 10, 14, . . .] [7]. Since e is
transcendental [6], so is σ(e − 2). The arithmetic CF arises because σ transforms the irregular
CF of e − 2 into the fractional part of coth(1/2). This provides a natural interpretation:
σ “regularizes” the Euler pattern [0; 1, 2, 1, 1, 4, . . .] via Möbius conjugation to hyperbolic
functions.
(6) The identities σ(tanh t) = e−2t and σ(i tan θ) = e−2iθ suggest a deep connection to Fourier/Hartley
analysis. Since σ(tan θ) =√ tan(π/4 − θ), the involution acts as angle reflection around
π/8, where its fixed point
