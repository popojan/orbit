# Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel (1784–1846)

**Type:** Learning document
**Subject:** Biography of Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel
**Sources:** MacTutor History of Mathematics, Britannica, Wikipedia, verified via web search

---

## Overview

Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel was a German astronomer and mathematician who made foundational contributions to both fields. He is best known for:
- The first reliable measurement of stellar parallax (61 Cygni, 1838)
- Systematic development of Bessel functions
- Establishing rigorous methods in observational astronomy

---

## Life

### Birth and Early Years

- **Born:** July 22, 1784, Minden, Westphalia (then part of Brandenburg-Prussia)
- **Died:** March 17, 1846, Königsberg, Prussia (now Kaliningrad, Russia)
- **Parents:** Carl Friedrich Bessel (government official) and Friederike Ernestine Schrader
- **Siblings:** Eight (Friedrich was the second of nine children)

### Education and Self-Teaching

Bessel left school at age 14 to become an apprentice at a trading company in Bremen (Kulenkamp & Söhne, 1799–1806). His interest in navigation and trade routes led him to study:
- Arithmetic and accounting (for business)
- Navigation (for cargo ships)
- Geography and languages (Spanish, English)
- Astronomy and mathematics (self-taught, starting ~1804)

**Key turning point:** While learning navigation, Bessel became interested in determining ship positions from stellar observations. He taught himself spherical geometry, astronomy, and eventually celestial mechanics.

### The Halley's Comet Calculation (1804)

At age 20, Bessel independently reduced observations of Halley's Comet from 1607 by Thomas Harriot and calculated the comet's orbit. He sent his work to Heinrich Wilhelm Olbers, a prominent Bremen physician and astronomer.

**Olbers's response:** Deeply impressed, Olbers encouraged Bessel to continue astronomical work and helped him publish the paper in *Monatliche Correspondenz* (1804).

### Career at Lilienthal Observatory (1806–1810)

On Olbers's recommendation, Bessel became assistant to Johann Hieronymus Schröter at the private Lilienthal Observatory near Bremen. Here he:
- Made systematic observations of comets and planets
- Began his monumental work on James Bradley's observations
- Developed expertise in precision astronomical measurement

### Königsberg Observatory (1810–1846)

In 1810, at age 25, Bessel was appointed:
- **Director of the new Königsberg Observatory** (construction supervised by Bessel himself)
- **Professor of Astronomy at the Albertus University of Königsberg**

He remained in Königsberg for the rest of his life, establishing it as one of Europe's premier observatories.

**Honorary doctorate:** The University of Göttingen awarded Bessel a doctorate honoris causa in 1811, as he had never attended university.

### Family

- **Married:** Johanna Hagen (1812)
- **Children:** Two sons and three daughters
- One daughter married Georg Adolf Erman (physicist and explorer)

## Major Scientific Contributions

### 1. Fundamenta Astronomiae (1818)

Bessel's reduction of 3,222 stellar positions from James Bradley's 18th-century observations, corrected for:
- Atmospheric refraction
- Instrumental errors
- Proper motion of stars
- Aberration and nutation

This work established the foundation of modern positional astronomy and set standards for precision that influenced all subsequent star catalogs.

### 2. Stellar Parallax of 61 Cygni (1838)

**The Problem:** Since Copernicus (1543), astronomers tried to measure stellar parallax (the apparent shift in a star's position due to Earth's orbit around the Sun). Failure to detect it was a major argument against heliocentrism.

**Bessel's approach:**
- Selected 61 Cygni, a binary star with high proper motion (suggesting proximity)
- Used the Fraunhofer heliometer (a precision instrument for measuring small angles)
- Measured the star's position relative to background stars over 18 months

**Result:** Parallax of 0.314 arcseconds (modern value: 0.287"), corresponding to a distance of about 10 light-years.

**Published:** December 1838 in *Astronomische Nachrichten*

**Significance:** First reliable measurement of stellar distance, confirming the vast scale of the universe and providing definitive proof of Earth's motion.

**Note:** Thomas Henderson (South Africa) and Friedrich Georg Wilhelm von Struve (Russia) made similar measurements around the same time, but Bessel published first with the most rigorous analysis.

### 3. Bessel Functions (1817, 1824)

While studying perturbations in planetary motion (the Kepler problem), Bessel systematically investigated the functions now named after him:

$$J_n(x) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\pi \cos(n\tau - x\sin\tau) \, d\tau$$

**Key works:**
- "Analytische Auflösung der Kepler'schen Aufgabe" (1817)
- "Untersuchung des Theils der planetarischen Störungen" (1824)

#### Historical Priority: Bernoulli, Euler, and Bessel

The functions bearing Bessel's name were encountered by earlier mathematicians in different physical contexts:

**Daniel Bernoulli (1738):** In his study of oscillations of a hanging chain, Bernoulli derived what we now recognize as Bessel functions of order zero. He expressed the solution as an infinite series but did not develop a general theory.

**Leonhard Euler (1764):** Euler encountered these functions while analyzing the vibrations of a circular drumhead (the 2D wave equation in polar coordinates). In his paper "De motu vibratorio tympanorum" he derived series solutions that are Bessel functions, again without recognizing them as a distinct class of functions.

**Friedrich Bessel (1817–1824):** Bessel's contribution was not discovering these functions but providing:
- A unified treatment connecting previously isolated occurrences
- General recurrence relations and differential equation
- Systematic study of zeros and asymptotic behavior
- Tables of numerical values for practical computation

**Why "Bessel" functions?** The naming reflects mathematical convention: functions are often named not for who first encountered them, but for who systematically developed their theory. Compare: "Gaussian" distribution (known to de Moivre), "Fourier" series (known to Euler).

**Chronological gap:** Euler died in 1783; Bessel was born in 1784. They never met, yet Bessel built upon Euler's mathematical legacy, transforming scattered observations into a coherent theory half a century later.

**Applications today:**
- Wave propagation (acoustics, electromagnetics)
- Heat conduction in cylinders
- Quantum mechanics (hydrogen atom)
- Signal processing

### 4. Geodesy and Earth's Shape

Bessel conducted extensive geodetic surveys and in 1841 determined the "Bessel ellipsoid" - an approximation of Earth's shape with:
- Equatorial radius: 6,377,397 m
- Polar radius: 6,356,079 m
- Flattening: 1/299.15

This ellipsoid was used as the reference for many national geodetic surveys until the 20th century.

### 5. Personal Equation

Bessel discovered that different observers consistently record the timing of astronomical events differently. This systematic error, which he called the "personal equation," revolutionized the understanding of measurement error and led to psychological studies of reaction time.

## Scientific Context and Contemporaries

### Königsberg Circle

Bessel was part of a remarkable group at Königsberg:
- **Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi** (1804–1851) - mathematician (elliptic functions)
- **Franz Ernst Neumann** (1798–1895) - physicist (crystallography, electromagnetics)
- **Heinrich Friedrich Weber** - astronomer

### Correspondence and Collaboration

| Scientist | Connection |
|-----------|------------|
| Wilhelm Olbers | Mentor, lifelong correspondent |
| Carl Friedrich Gauss | Frequent correspondence on astronomy and geodesy |
| Friedrich Georg Wilhelm von Struve | Parallel stellar parallax work |
| Joseph von Fraunhofer | Provided precision instruments (heliometer) |
| Alexander von Humboldt | Friend, supported Bessel's work |

### The Fraunhofer Connection

Joseph von Fraunhofer (1787–1826), the Bavarian optician, created the heliometer that made Bessel's parallax measurement possible. Fraunhofer's instruments represented the state of the art in optical precision.

## The Sirius Companion (1844)

In 1844, Bessel announced that Sirius (the brightest star) showed a "wobble" in its proper motion, indicating an unseen companion. He predicted:
- Orbital period: ~50 years
- Companion mass: comparable to the Sun

**Verification:** In 1862, Alvan Graham Clark discovered Sirius B, a white dwarf, exactly where Bessel predicted. This was the first detection of a star via gravitational effects alone.

Bessel made a similar prediction for Procyon (also confirmed posthumously).

## Death and Legacy

### Final Years

Bessel suffered from deteriorating health in his later years. He died on March 17, 1846, in Königsberg, likely from cancer.

### Honors and Memorials

- **Lunar crater Bessel** (15.5 km diameter)
- **Asteroid 1552 Bessel**
- **Bessel functions** bear his name
- **Bessell ellipsoid** used in geodesy

### Impact on Astronomy

Bessel established:
- Rigorous standards for observational accuracy
- Methods for systematic error analysis
- The importance of instrument calibration

His approach influenced all subsequent precision astronomy.
