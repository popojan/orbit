# Leonhard Euler (1707–1783)

**Type:** Learning document
**Subject:** Biography of Leonhard Euler
**Sources:** MacTutor History of Mathematics, Britannica, Wikipedia, Euler Archive, verified via web search

---

## Overview

Leonhard Euler was the most prolific mathematician in history, authoring over 800 papers and books. His contributions span virtually every area of mathematics known in his time, and he introduced much of the notation still used today. Despite becoming completely blind in his later years, his productivity only increased.

---

## Life

### Birth and Family

- **Born:** April 15, 1707, Basel, Switzerland
- **Died:** September 18, 1783, St. Petersburg, Russia
- **Father:** Paul III Euler (1670–1745), pastor of the Reformed Church
- **Mother:** Marguerite Brucker (1677–1761), from a family of scholars

Shortly after Leonhard's birth, the family moved to Riehen, near Basel, where his father served as pastor. Paul Euler had studied mathematics under Jacob Bernoulli and gave his son early instruction in the subject.

### Education at Basel

- **1720:** Enrolled at University of Basel at age 13
- **1723:** Master of Philosophy (thesis comparing Descartes and Newton)
- **1726:** Completed dissertation on sound propagation

**The Bernoulli connection:** Euler's father wanted him to become a pastor, but Johann Bernoulli (1667–1748), who had succeeded his brother Jacob as professor of mathematics at Basel, recognized Euler's exceptional talent. Bernoulli arranged private Saturday afternoon lessons and convinced Paul Euler to let his son pursue mathematics.

Euler later wrote: "I was given permission to visit [Johann Bernoulli] freely every Saturday afternoon and he kindly explained to me everything I could not understand."

### First St. Petersburg Period (1727–1741)

In 1727, Euler accepted a position at the newly established St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences, where Johann Bernoulli's sons Daniel and Nicolaus were already working.

**Career progression:**
- 1727: Arrived in St. Petersburg; initially assigned to medical department
- 1731: Professor of physics
- 1733: Professor of mathematics (succeeding Daniel Bernoulli, who returned to Basel)

**Health crisis (1735):** Euler nearly died from a severe fever, possibly related to overwork on cartographic projects for the Academy. This may have contributed to his later vision problems.

**First vision loss (1738):** Euler lost most sight in his right eye, which he attributed to strain from cartographic work. He reportedly said: "Now I will have fewer distractions."

### Marriage and Family

- **1734:** Married Katharina Gsell (1707–1773), daughter of painter Georg Gsell
- **Children:** 13 born, 5 survived to adulthood (3 sons, 2 daughters)
- **1776:** After Katharina's death, married her half-sister Salome Abigail Gsell

Euler was devoted to his family and often worked with a child on his lap.

### Berlin Period (1741–1766)

Political instability in Russia (regency struggles after Empress Anna's death) prompted Euler to accept an invitation from Frederick II of Prussia to help establish the Berlin Academy of Sciences.

**Achievements in Berlin:**
- 1741: Arrived in Berlin
- 1744: Director of mathematics at the Academy
- 1748: Published *Introductio in analysin infinitorum*
- 1755: Published *Institutiones calculi differentialis*
- 1759: Assumed leadership of the Academy after Maupertuis's death (though never officially president)

**Productivity:** During his 25 years in Berlin, Euler published approximately 380 papers—roughly one every three weeks.

**Letters to a German Princess:** Euler tutored Friederike Charlotte of Brandenburg-Schwedt (Frederick's niece). His 200+ letters to her, explaining scientific concepts in accessible language, were later published and became one of the most popular science books of the 18th century.

**Conflict with Frederick:** The relationship deteriorated over time. Frederick, an Enlightenment philosopher who admired French culture, found the devout, practical Euler unsophisticated compared to Voltaire and other French intellectuals at court. Frederick mockingly called Euler "my Cyclops" (referring to his lost eye). When Frederick offered the Academy presidency to d'Alembert in 1763, Euler decided to leave.

### Second St. Petersburg Period (1766–1783)

Catherine the Great invited Euler to return to Russia with generous terms. He accepted in 1766, to Frederick's anger.

**Complete blindness:** Shortly after returning, a cataract was discovered in his left eye. An operation in 1771 temporarily restored some vision, but within days Euler was totally blind.

**Astonishing productivity:** Despite complete blindness, Euler's output increased. With the help of assistants (including his sons Johann Albrecht and Christoph) who wrote from his dictation, he produced nearly half of his lifetime output during this final period—approximately one paper per week in 1775.

Euler had an extraordinary memory. He could recite the entire *Aeneid* and perform complex calculations mentally, dictating results to his scribes.

### Death

On September 18, 1783, Euler spent the morning calculating the orbit of the newly discovered planet Uranus with Anders Johan Lexell. After lunch with his family, he suffered a brain hemorrhage and died within hours. He was 76.

**Condorcet's eulogy:** "He ceased to calculate and to live."

**Burial:** Originally buried in St. Petersburg; in 1957 (250th anniversary of his birth) his remains were moved to the Lazarevskoe Cemetery at the Alexander Nevsky Monastery.

## Major Mathematical Contributions

### Mathematical Notation

Euler standardized much of modern mathematical notation:

| Symbol | Meaning | Euler's contribution |
|--------|---------|---------------------|
| *e* | Base of natural logarithm | First systematic use (1727/1736) |
| π | Ratio of circumference to diameter | Popularized (not invented) |
| *i* | √(−1) | Introduced (1777) |
| Σ | Summation | Introduced |
| *f(x)* | Function notation | Introduced (1734) |
| sin, cos, tan | Trigonometric functions | Modern notation |

### The Basel Problem (1735)

The problem: Find the exact value of $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2}$

This had been open since Pietro Mengoli posed it in 1650. The Bernoullis and other leading mathematicians had failed to solve it.

**Euler's solution:** $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$

Euler announced his solution at age 28 (presented December 5, 1735), bringing him immediate fame. His proof, while brilliant, was not rigorous by modern standards—it took Weierstrass a century later to fill the gaps.

Euler generalized this to all even powers, computing $\zeta(2k)$ for arbitrary positive integers $k$.

### Continued Fractions (1737)

In "De fractionibus continuis dissertatio" (presented March 7, 1737; published 1744), Euler developed the first comprehensive theory of continued fractions.

**Key results:**
- Continued fraction expansion for *e*: [2; 1, 2, 1, 1, 4, 1, 1, 6, 1, 1, 8, ...]
- Connection between continued fractions and the Riccati equation
- Methods for transforming infinite series into continued fractions
- First proof that *e* is irrational (implicit in the CF structure)

**Connection to e-spiral:** The continued fraction for coth(1/2) = (e+1)/(e−1), which equals [2; 6, 10, 14, 18, ...] (arithmetic progression), was known to Euler. This CF appears in OEIS A016825 and relates to Bessel functions through the connection formula.

### Number Theory

- **Euler's totient function φ(n):** Count of integers ≤n coprime to n
- **Fermat's little theorem:** Generalized and proved
- **Two squares theorem:** Proved Fermat's claim that primes ≡1 (mod 4) are sums of two squares
- **Quadratic reciprocity:** Conjectured (later proved by Gauss)
- **Partition function:** Foundational work with generating functions

### Analysis

- **Euler's identity:** $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$ (connecting five fundamental constants)
- **Euler's formula:** $e^{ix} = \cos x + i \sin x$
- **Gamma function:** Extended factorials to non-integers
- **Zeta function:** Studied $\zeta(s) = \sum n^{-s}$, proved the Euler product formula

### Graph Theory and Topology

- **Seven Bridges of Königsberg (1736):** Proved no Eulerian path exists; founded graph theory
- **Euler characteristic:** V − E + F = 2 for convex polyhedra

### Physics and Applied Mathematics

- **Euler equations** of fluid dynamics
- **Euler-Lagrange equation** in calculus of variations
- **Rigid body dynamics:** Euler angles, Euler's rotation theorem
- **Buckling of columns:** Euler's critical load formula

### Bessel Functions

In "De motu vibratorio tympanorum" (1764), Euler studied vibrations of a circular drumhead. His series solutions are what we now call Bessel functions of the first kind.

**Historical note:** Euler (1764) and Daniel Bernoulli (1738) encountered these functions before Bessel was born (1784). Bessel's systematic treatment (1817–1824) unified these scattered results into a coherent theory. See: `docs/learning/bessel-biography.md`

## Complete Works

### The *Opera Omnia*

The project to publish Euler's complete works began in 1911 and is still ongoing. As of 2023:

| Series | Content | Volumes |
|--------|---------|---------|
| I | Mathematics | 29 volumes |
| II | Mechanics and astronomy | 31 volumes |
| III | Physics and miscellaneous | 12 volumes |
| IVA | Correspondence | 10+ volumes |

**Total:** Over 70 volumes, approximately 25,000 pages.

## Legacy

### Why Euler Matters

1. **Volume:** More mathematical output than anyone before or since
2. **Breadth:** Contributed to virtually every branch of mathematics
3. **Notation:** Created the language we still use
4. **Accessibility:** *Letters to a German Princess* showed science could be explained clearly
5. **Resilience:** Produced half his work while completely blind

### Named After Euler

- Euler's number (*e*)
- Euler's identity, formula, method, angles, characteristic, ...
- Eulerian path/circuit
- Euler–Maclaurin formula
- Euler–Lagrange equation
- Asteroid 2002 Euler
- Lunar crater Euler

**Laplace allegedly said:** "Read Euler, read Euler, he is the master of us all."
