# Édouard Zeckendorf (1901-1983): The Amateur Who Named a Theorem

## The Doctor-Mathematician

Édouard Zeckendorf was born on **2 May 1901** in **Liège, Belgium**, and died there on **16 May 1983**. He was not a professional mathematician but a medical doctor and Belgian Army officer who pursued mathematics as a passionate amateur.

## Family Background

Zeckendorf's parents were **Abraham Zeckendorf** and **Henriette van Gelder**. His father was a dentist who established a dental practice in Liège near the end of the 19th century. The family was Dutch, and Abraham Zeckendorf was a practicing Jew.

## Education

Zeckendorf entered the **Royal Athenaeum of Liège** in 1912, where he studied classical languages (Greek and Latin), modern languages (English and German), as well as mathematics and drawing. He excelled particularly in mathematics and drawing, subjects he greatly loved throughout his life.

In **1925**, Zeckendorf graduated as a medical doctor from the **University of Liège** and immediately joined the Belgian Army medical corps. By **1930**, he was in charge of the **Saint Laurent Hospital** in Liège, a military hospital.

## World War II

When Germany invaded Belgium in **1940**, Zeckendorf was captured and remained a **prisoner of war until 1945**. During his captivity, he provided medical care to other Allied POWs, demonstrating the same dedication he showed throughout his career.

For his wartime service, Zeckendorf received numerous honors:
- Officer of the Order of the Oak Crown (1946)
- Prisoner of War Medal (1946)
- Officer of the Order of Leopold (1949)
- Officer of the Order of Leopold II (1950)

## The Theorem

Zeckendorf's theorem states:

> **Every positive integer has a unique representation as a sum of non-consecutive Fibonacci numbers.**

For example:
- 10 = 8 + 2 = F₆ + F₃
- 17 = 13 + 3 + 1 = F₇ + F₄ + F₂
- 100 = 89 + 8 + 3 = F₁₁ + F₆ + F₄

The "non-consecutive" constraint (no two adjacent Fibonacci numbers in the sum) ensures uniqueness.

## The Publication Gap

Zeckendorf had obtained his theorem and related results **by 1939**, but before he could publish, World War II broke out. He was captured in 1940 and spent five years as a POW.

He finally published his results in **1972**, over three decades after discovery. Ironically, his work was not entirely original by then—in accordance with **Stigler's Law of Eponymy** (which states that no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer), Zeckendorf's theorem had been independently discovered by others. The Belgian mathematician **C.G. Lekkerkerker** had published a proof in 1952.

Nevertheless, Zeckendorf's name became attached to the theorem, and it is now universally known as "Zeckendorf's theorem" or "Zeckendorf representation."

## Legacy

MathSciNet lists over **50 papers** with Zeckendorf's name in the title, appearing as:
- Zeckendorf numbers
- Zeckendorf representations
- Zeckendorf decompositions
- Zeckendorf trees
- Zeckendorf arrays
- Zeckendorf identities
- Zeckendorf expansions

Over 100 additional papers reference his work in reviews. The representation he named has found applications in:
- Fibonacci coding (data compression)
- Computer science algorithms
- Number theory research
- Combinatorics

## Personal Life

Zeckendorf married **Elsa Schwers** in 1929. By 1931, he had added dental surgery to his qualifications. Tragically, Elsa died in **1944** while he was still a prisoner of war—he learned of her death in captivity.

He retired from the army in **1957** at the rank of **Colonel**. That same year, his mathematical work was recognized when the Société Royale des Sciences de Liège elected him as an associate member.

In 1959, he married **Marie Jeanne Lempereur**. She predeceased him in 1977. Zeckendorf died in Liège on 16 May 1983, just two weeks after his 82nd birthday.

## Mathematical Publications

After the war, Zeckendorf published over **30 mathematical papers**. His early work appeared in *Mathesis*:
- "Étude fibonaccienne. Arrangements avec répétition de lettres a et de chaînes limitées de lettres b" (1949)
- "Étude fibonaccienne. De certaines coupes obliques parallèles dans les polytopes arithmétiques à (p−1) dimensions" (1950)
- "Étude fibonaccienne" (1951)

Many later papers appeared in the *Bulletin de la Société Royale des Sciences de Liège*.

## The Amateur's Lesson

Zeckendorf's story illustrates that significant mathematical discoveries can come from unexpected sources. A Belgian army doctor, captured as a POW, discovered a theorem about Fibonacci numbers that now bears his name in hundreds of papers. His 33-year gap between discovery and publication—interrupted by war and professional duties—reminds us that mathematical ideas transcend circumstances.
