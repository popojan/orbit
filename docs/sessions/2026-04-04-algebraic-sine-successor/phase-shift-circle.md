# Phase Shift and the Hidden Circle

**Date:** 2026-04-04
**Status:** ✅ PROVEN (polynomial identity)

## Setup

Transfer matrix (fixed, determines the recurrence):

$$M = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det M = 1, \qquad c = \frac{\alpha}{2} = \cos\theta$$

State vector (carries the initial conditions):

$$v_k = M^k \, v_0 = \begin{pmatrix} f_{k+1} \\ f_k \end{pmatrix}$$

The orbit value $f_k$ is always the **second component** of $v_k$.

## Two Basis Solutions — Same Matrix, Different IC

The recurrence $f_{k+1} = \alpha f_k - f_{k-1}$ has two linearly independent solutions,
distinguished solely by the initial vector $v_0$:

| Solution | Name | $v_0$ | $f_k$ | Trigonometric |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cosine** | $C_k = T_k(c)$ | $\begin{pmatrix} c \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ | Chebyshev 1st kind | $\cos(k\theta)$ |
| **Sine** | $S_k = U_{k-1}(c)$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ | Chebyshev 2nd kind | $\displaystyle\frac{\sin(k\theta)}{\sin\theta}$ |

Both are generated by the **same matrix** $M$. The only difference is $v_0$.

## The Circle: Pythagorean Identity

The two solutions satisfy a polynomial identity in $c$ (proven by expanding
Chebyshev recurrences, verified for all $k$):

$$\boxed{T_k(c)^2 + (1 - c^2)\, U_{k-1}(c)^2 = 1}$$

Equivalently, with $\sin\theta = \sqrt{1 - c^2}$:

$$\cos^2(k\theta) + \sin^2(k\theta) = 1$$

The pair $\big(T_k(c),\; \sin\theta \cdot U_{k-1}(c)\big)$ traces the **unit circle**
for all $k$. This is exact, not approximate.

## Extracting the Circle from a Single Orbit

Given any orbit with initial conditions $f_0, f_1$, we can decompose:

$$f_k = f_0 \, C_k + (f_1 - f_0\, c) \, S_k$$

The $\pi/2$-shifted companion signal, constructed from consecutive orbit values:

$$g_k = \frac{c \cdot f_k - f_{k-1}}{\sqrt{1 - c^2}}$$

Then for all $k$:

$$\boxed{f_k^2 + g_k^2 = A^2}$$

where the constant amplitude is:

$$A^2 = \frac{f_0^2 + f_1^2 - \alpha\, f_0 f_1}{1 - c^2} = \frac{Q(f_1, f_0)}{1 - c^2}$$

Here $Q(x, y) = x^2 - \alpha\, xy + y^2$ is the conserved quadratic form of $M$.

## The Change of Basis

The matrix $L$ that reveals the circle:

$$L = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ \frac{1}{\sin\theta} & -\frac{c}{\sin\theta} \end{pmatrix}$$

maps the state vector to circular coordinates:

$$L \begin{pmatrix} f_{k+1} \\ f_k \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} f_k \\ g_k \end{pmatrix}$$

In these coordinates, the transfer matrix becomes a **pure rotation**:

$$L \, M \, L^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$$

The orbit, which is an **ellipse** in $(f_{k+1}, f_k)$ coordinates,
becomes a **circle** in $(f_k, g_k)$ coordinates.

## Why the Standard Coordinates See an Ellipse

The state $(f_{k+1}, f_k)$ represents two consecutive samples of the same sine wave.
Using the product-to-sum formula:

$$f_{k+1} \cdot f_k = A^2 \sin(k\theta + \phi)\sin((k+1)\theta + \phi)$$

This cross-product oscillates, which is why $f_{k+1}^2 + f_k^2$ is not constant —
the two components are not in quadrature. They are separated by angle $\theta$,
not $\pi/2$.

The form $Q$ corrects for this: the $-\alpha\, xy$ term subtracts the cross-correlation
introduced by the non-orthogonal sampling.

## Matrix Generalization

Since $T_k(c)^2 + (1 - c^2)\, U_{k-1}(c)^2 = 1$ is a **polynomial identity** in $c$,
it holds for $c = \alpha/2$ where $\alpha$ is a matrix in any ring $R$.

Replace:

| Scalar | Matrix ($\alpha \in \mathrm{Mat}_n(R)$) |
|---|---|
| $c = \alpha/2$ | $c = \alpha/2$ (matrix) |
| $\sin\theta = \sqrt{1 - c^2}$ | $\Sigma = \sqrt{I - \alpha^2/4}$ (matrix square root) |
| $T_k(c) \in \mathbb{R}$ | $T_k(c) \in \mathrm{Mat}_n(R)$ |
| $U_{k-1}(c) \in \mathbb{R}$ | $U_{k-1}(c) \in \mathrm{Mat}_n(R)$ |

The identity becomes:

$$T_k(c)^2 + \Sigma^2 \, U_{k-1}(c)^2 = I$$

This holds because:
1. All terms are polynomials in $\alpha$ → they commute with each other
2. Polynomial identities are preserved under ring homomorphisms

The matrix square root $\Sigma = \sqrt{I - \alpha^2/4}$ exists and is unique (positive definite)
when all eigenvalues of $\alpha$ lie in $(-2, 2)$ — the fully oscillatory regime.

## Connection to the Triptych

| Regime | $\|c\|$ | Circle exists? | $\Sigma^2 = I - c^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oscillatory | $< 1$ | Yes — orbit on circle | Positive definite |
| Degenerate | $= 1$ | Circle collapses to a line | $= 0$ (singular) |
| Hyperbolic | $> 1$ | No — orbit on hyperbola | Negative definite |

At the degenerate point $c = 1$: $\sin\theta = 0$, the circle has zero "width"
in the $g$ direction. The 2D circular motion collapses to 1D linear motion —
counting the naturals.
